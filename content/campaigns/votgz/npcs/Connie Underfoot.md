# Constance "Connie" Leah Underfoot -- "The Factor"

## What this document is

The complete reference for the Factor: who she is, what the business does, how
the party ended up in it, and how to run her. It supersedes the earlier handoff
document and stands alone. Nothing else needs reading first.

Sections are marked **CANON** (fixed, do not contradict), **PROPOSED**
(accepted in discussion, revisable), or **OPEN** (deliberately unanswered).

---

## In one line

A halfling fortune-teller who worked out that the con had been an
apprenticeship, and is now three steps into a much longer climb.

---

## CANON -- fixed points

**Name.** Constance "Connie" Leah Underfoot. The joke is "Constantly Under
Foot." She knows.

**Ancestry.** Halfling.

**She is not a villain.** Chaotic good. She is the party's employer, not their
antagonist. This matters more than anything else in this document. Earlier
drafts of her art and description kept drifting sinister and had to be pulled
back every time.

**Telekinesis is her signature.** She never reaches for anything and never
raises a hand to make a point. In the original 5E build she was a Charlatan
Bard of a homebrew "College of Fortune": social skills, Mage Hand, Telekinetic
Shove, and a deck of Tarokka cards redrawn at random after every long rest. The
PF2E equivalent is a **Psychic** on **The Distant Grasp** conscious mind, occult
tradition, telekinetic, with *Kinetic Ram*, *Telekinetic Maneuver*, *Gravity
Well* and an amped *Telekinetic Projectile*. PF2E has both **Charlatan** and
**Fortune Teller** backgrounds; either fits.

**Fortune-telling is her origin and her public cover**, not a phase she grew out
of. The brokerage is the front's front.

**Her goal is power.** The brokerage is one stop, not a career. She intends to
become a Power in the underworld. The debts build capital, but the *network* is
the real product: people who, once clear of the Book, still know her and still
take her work.

**She has appeared on Xuartlek before.** In an earlier campaign the same player
ran, Connie was the party's employer at roughly **17th level**. First session:
the players converged on her one at a time; once they had all arrived, so did
the law. The building surrounded, a storming party forming up, airships
overhead. She glanced out of the window, snapped her fingers, and two airships
collided in mid-air and came down on the cordon below. She said **"Ooops!"** and
walked the party through a portal to another world.

That campaign folded and the setting has changed enormously since. Treat that
scene as **her future, not her past**: foreshadowing available to you, not
current capability.

**Current power level: roughly 7th.** Meaningfully beyond the party, nowhere
near the archmage of that scene. This ceiling exists specifically so she cannot
solve the campaign.

## The business

### What a factor is

Connie buys bad paper cheap. Debts nobody expects to collect, usually owed by
people with a useful skill and no way to pay. Those debtors go on the Book, and
the Book is the whole business.

She does not employ them, and she does not own them in any sense she would
defend. She *holds their paper*, and she rents out their time until the paper is
clear. Clients across the empire post work they cannot be seen to do, bidders
compete for a crew, and the Factor takes the highest offer, deducts her costs and
her margin, and credits the remainder against what the crew owes.

She is small. One broker in Lapurrak with a clerk, a modest book and a leased
portal allocation. The reach belongs to the doors and the clients, not to her.

### The Book

A ledger of money and a wall of cards of people, and she treats them as one
system. Money in the columns, a card for every person who has ever been on the
Book, filed in the index drawers behind her desk. She keeps the card after the
debt clears. That is the point of keeping it.

The number on the Book is honest. She will state it, unprompted, at first
meeting, along with what she paid for it and the fact that the two are different.
She will not say how much less she paid. That spread is her margin and she is
straight about having one.

**The arithmetic.** The Book carries **what she paid, plus a stated margin, plus
per-mission transit costs as they accrue**, not the ruinous face value of the
original debt. A factor who buys at a sixth and collects the whole is a predator.
A factor who buys at a sixth and collects a third is a business, and only the
second version survives contact with "crews genuinely get free." This is the
single fact that makes her chaotic good: the empire destroyed these people, and
Connie is the discount.

| | gp | |
|---|---|---|
| Face value assessed by the empire | 34,261 | discharged by the sale |
| Her winning bid, ship and crew as one lot | 6,000 | 17.5% of face |
| **The Book** | **12,000** | 35% of face, **6.7x their net worth** |

6,000 gp is far beyond a broker who lives hand to mouth on opening fees, so **she
borrowed to buy them**. Keep that bind: she cannot afford to be gentle with the
schedule, because somebody is charging her interest on the four of them. It is why
the work never stops, and it never requires the party to meet her creditor.

Full schedule, fees and reputation bands are in the Book arc file. Short version:
four arcs of three sessions, one per player in the GM chair, clears it at 5th
level.

### The Call

The clerk posts a lot. Bidders make offers. The crew are told where to be and
when. They do not choose the job and they cannot decline it.

They are never told who the client is. Same rule, opposite reason to the one
people assume: if you do not know who hired you, nobody can make you say. She
tells crews that to their faces.

**Their reputation is a price, not a relationship.** Work cleanly and a crew
fetches more next time, which clears the Book faster. Make a mess and they fetch
less and it takes longer. There is no faction standing, no favour economy and
nobody to keep sweet. Just a figure that moves.

She shows them the arithmetic every time. Fee, costs, margin, remainder, new
balance.

### The doors

What makes her worth bidding on is portal access.

She leases openings from the research body House Haritza created, the one that
officially holds one working portal ring and the wreckage of three. The rings are
**person-gauge**: one person and what they can carry, no hardware needed on the
far side. That means she can put a crew on any world in the empire, or off the
edge of it, with no manifest, no customs and no record that anyone travelled.

Ship-gauge transit is a different thing entirely and needs an anchor at both
ends, which is why the empire's registered portal network still matters and why
freight still moves the slow legal way. Every job therefore runs in two legs:
**the crew go through the ring, and the heavy end travels by ship on the public
network, weeks later, with a manifest.** For the party that means arriving with
what they can carry and no way home until the *Grim Zephyr* makes port.

**She pays per opening, in advance.** The return is **reserved in advance with a
deposit and confirmed on signal**. Both cost her real money before a crew has
earned a penny, which is why she is permanently long on people and short on cash,
why she cannot scale, and why she cannot buy her way out of trouble.

It is also why she wants crews home alive, for reasons that are entirely
financial and completely indistinguishable from caring. Nobody, including her,
needs to work out which it is.

### Enforcement

She has no muscle and never needs any. The market does it for her.

**Fail badly, or refuse to work, and she sells your card on**, at a discount, to
somebody who does keep muscle. That is the entire threat and she will explain it
plainly, without heat, in the first meeting. Her pitch is honest and horrible at
once: *she is the nicest owner your debt is ever going to have*, and she is
correct.

**CANON -- the other thing.** Betray a client, or betray her, and she is capable
of a great deal worse, and has been twice. She does not advertise this and never
alludes to it. A threat that is never spoken cannot deter anyone, so it is not
leverage; it is a decision she makes alone and then keeps.

**PROPOSED -- the mechanism.** She does not kill anyone. She declines to complete
a transaction. The return opening is a reservation she has to actively confirm,
and she has ring access to worlds that are on no chart. It costs her a deposit
and an afternoon of not doing something, and there is no body because there is no
place to look.

The arithmetic she is actually doing is not about her pride. Betrayal ends the
standing arrangement with her landlord; no arrangement means no openings, no
openings means no business, and no business means every card in that wall gets
sold to somebody who keeps muscle. The question is one person or forty. Chaotic
good is entirely comfortable answering it. Lawful characters would need a
process.

**She keeps their cards**, filed in the same drawers as everyone who ever cleared
the Book, in no particular order, and she can tell you which ones they are.

## CANON -- how the party came to be on the Book

Nobody rescued them. They walked into it, and every step was legitimate.

They were not found. They were **cross-referenced**.

1. The *Grim Zephyr* came through the Issoire portal showing obvious damage and
   without paying the 100 gp toll. Non-payment, statutory penalties and a daily
   failure-to-pay fee begin accruing from that day.
2. Separately, the party sold a captured prize sloop at Sielmoro and did not pay
   the duty on the sale.
3. **Pender Holst came to collect that duty**, crossing a sea to Thelonésë to do
   it. Collecting it meant opening the sellers' file. The file had another hull on
   it: *Grim Zephyr*, registry unresolved, logged inbound at Issoire and never
   logged out.
4. He filed a note. The note raised the derelict flag. **Their own paperwork did
   this**, and the man who did it was being conscientious.
5. Provident Salvage of Issoire went looking, found her at Thelonésë, and offered
   900 gp to tow her home. An honest price, honestly meant. Accepting it
   crystallised a statutory salvage award of 8,000 gp that nobody mentioned,
   because it is a claim against the vessel filed with the Office and not the
   salvor's to discuss.
6. Halloran's Yard repaired the crystal for 2,400 gp. A fair price, good work.
7. Then the **Transit Office** presented the consolidated statement: 34,261 gp 2
   sp. It annihilated every option they had, including the loan.
8. Ship and crew were seized and sold at the debtors' market. **Connie bought.**

The toll was one hundred gold. Everything else is the Office's opinion about being
paid late. The full itemised statement is a player-facing prop; see
[[statement-of-account]].

Their Letter of Marque from Governor Thesalor is worth nothing here. It is an
instrument of the Principality of Zolnuth and the creditor is imperial. The
moment they try it and it fails is the moment the scale of the thing lands.

**PROPOSED -- nobody colluded.** The salvor was not lying, the yard was not lying,
and the Transit Office was doing its job. But everyone in Issoire has seen this
end this way fifty times, and the salvor simply did not mention the transit bill
because it was not his business. Asked afterwards whether he knew, he says yes,
of course, without a flicker of guilt. That is more horrible than a conspiracy
and it gives the party nobody to punch.

**PROPOSED -- the parties.** *Provident Salvage* of Issoire, whose name promises
foresight and whose entire trade is other people's lack of it. *Halloran's Yard*,
also Issoire, the only party in the whole affair who behaved well and whose
invoice is the smallest thing on the page. *The Transit Office*, imperial,
everywhere.

**Pender Holst is not cruel and not corrupt. He is correct.** He waives nothing,
including the 2 gp late penalty. The 3 gp 4 sp the players heard in session 18 was
true and was line 2 of a four-line demand for 278 gp 5 sp; combat started before
he got through the rest. He does not mention the derelict flag, because he filed
that note before he left and it is not his file any more. He would be genuinely
distressed by what happens next and entirely unable to see that he did anything
wrong, because he didn't.

**Title, so nobody relitigates it.** The original owner of the *Verdant Zephyr*
has no claim: they were made whole by underwriters, who took title by subrogation,
declared a constructive total loss and struck the registration, which is why the
hull matched no live registry entry. And the debtors' market sale was a judicial
sale under Office authority, which passes clean title free of all prior liens.
What survives instead is a claim file in an underwriter's office containing
statements from a helmsman, two cooks and a longshoreman. Nobody has
cross-referenced it. A cross-reference is what started all of this.

**PROPOSED -- the ship.** Connie bought hull and crew as one lot, so she owns the
*Grim Zephyr*, and she **re-registered her**, which is how the ship can now transit
portals lawfully at 100 gp a time. The party operate her. Clearing the Book
transfers the registry to them. Freedom is a document, and the win condition is an
object rather than an abstraction.

Implied hull value, from the statutory salvage award: **24,000 gp**. They stole a
24,000 gp airship and will pay 12,000 gp to own it lawfully. They are paying for
the *Verdant Zephyr*. They are just paying Connie.

## PROPOSED -- how a charlatan becomes a broker

There is no heist, no patron and no turning point. **The con was the
apprenticeship.**

A fortune teller learns who owes what to whom. People tell a fortune teller
things they would never tell a banker; that is the entire trade. She knew about
bad debts years before anyone thought to sell her one, and she already knew how
to value a person at a glance, because appraising people fast *was* the con.
Buying the first few debts was the most natural move available to her.

**The through-line is cards.** She read people off cards. Now she keeps people on
cards. The wall of index drawers behind her desk is the same instinct
industrialised, and it is visibly larger than the business needs.

## OPEN -- the question worth leaving unanswered

In her original conception the fortunes were *inconveniently true*: she sold the
comforting lie for coin and slipped the accurate warning in sideways.

Whether that is still so should stay open. Her desk has a tarot spread laid
directly across the columns of an open ledger, as though she stopped doing the
books to ask the cards about something in them. The ledger is for sums and the
cards are for people, and she lays a spread when the question is about a person.
Nobody, players included, should be able to tell whether that is superstition, an
old habit, or the actual decision-making process of the business.

---

## Manner

Never angry. Never threatens. **Never lies about the number.** That last is the
load-bearing one: it is why people are afraid of her, and it is also why she is
not evil. Nobody is frightened of Connie. They are frightened of the figure, and
she is the only person in the room who will tell them what it actually is.

Warm, and genuinely so. She is likeable, and being liked is a tool she has used
since she was young enough to need it. Faintly conspiratorial: she gives the
impression she is on your side of a joke about the situation. *You would trust
her, and you would be roughly half right.*

She must never read as cruel or smug. The unnerving thing about her is that she
is reasonable, correct, and completely unhurried.

## Physical

Small. Coppery red curls, loosely pinned and escaping at the temples. Fair,
freckled, green-hazel eyes, laugh lines. Dressed soberly and expensively:
charcoal wool coat over a high-collared ivory blouse, dark silk scarf, three good
rings, and worn a shade **too correctly**, with the precision of someone who
learned the costume rather than grew up in it. One button mismatched. Ink on her
right cuff.

## The office

Lapurrak, the thieves' quarter of the Under City, suspended beneath Xuartlek's
belly. Paperwork rather than muscle: ledgers, receipts, tea, a clerk, a queue.

- A wall of wooden index drawers, floor to ceiling, reached by a ladder. Far more
  of them than the business needs. Nobody reads that as ambition; they read it as
  sentimentality, which suits her fine.
- A brass desk lamp, a stack of papers, a small brass scale, a potted plant. A
  teapot that pours itself. A card held in mid-air with no hand near it.
- A battered painted fortune-teller's shingle from a much cheaper life, framed
  and propped on the desk. **It faces her.** Visitors mostly see the back of it.
- A window onto the Under City at night.

### Ambrose Sowerby, clerk

Human, and a full head taller than his employer, which reads correctly in the
room. He was an accounts clerk somewhere respectable and went on the Book over
something he did with a ledger. Connie bought his paper cheap because she needed
someone who could keep books. He cleared it in four years and never left.

He is the living proof that the term ends, which makes him the most valuable
thing in the office after the allocation.

**He says the number out loud** when a new crew arrives. Connie's view is that it
lands better from someone who has sat in that chair. It is also good business and
she would admit that if asked.

**He prepares the return confirmations and she signs them.** So he knows about the
two. He stayed anyway.

**OPEN -- whether he thinks she was right.**

## Campaign context

The party are privateers, 2nd level going on 3rd, whose airship the *Grim Zephyr*
Connie now owns. The campaign runs a **rotating GM** format hubbed in Xuartlek,
so the party composition changes between arcs.

Connie is played by Kevan when he runs. His player character, **John Jacob
Jingleheimer Schmidt**, a dwarf gunslinger of the Spellshot discipline, is
himself on the Book. Expect the two to share scenes.

### The players' brief, and the rule that follows from it

The players have asked for **no political gameplay**. They are content to work
for political entities; they do not want to play politics.

**All political consequence resolves at the broker, never at the party.** If a
job embarrasses a Great House, that is Connie's problem to smooth over. The only
thing the party ever see of it is next month's work being worse paid or nastier.
Politics is weather, expressed entirely through the job board. Nobody is ever
asked whose side they are on.

Her own ambition obeys the same rule. She is not building a faction, she is
building a Rolodex, and the party are in it. Her long game for them is that they
get free and still take her calls.

## Constraints for anyone writing her

1. **She must not be able to solve the party's problems.** Her leverage is
   commercial, not magical. When a scene invites "why doesn't Connie just fix
   this," the answer is that fixing it costs more than it is worth to her, or
   that her landlord will not wear it.
2. **She never gets her hands dirty and never needs to.** No muscle, no threats,
   no raised voice. The office is paperwork.
3. **Keep her chaotic good.** Renting out indentured labour reads badly written
   down; the trick is that she is straight about it. The number is honest, crews
   genuinely get free, and working cleanly genuinely shortens the term. None of
   the network-building works if she treats people badly.
4. **The debtors are not disposable to her, only to the clients.** She is
   building relationships she intends to still have in twenty years. A client who
   gets a crew killed does not bid again. That is procurement policy, and it is
   also the only protection she can actually offer.
5. **The two she did not bring home are never mentioned by her.** Not as a
   threat, not as a warning, not as a confession. If the players learn it, they
   learn it from somewhere else.

## Running her when you are not Kevan

Five fixed points, so that five different GMs produce the same woman:

1. She is never angry and never in a hurry.
2. She never threatens, because she has never needed to.
3. She never lies about the number, and she volunteers it before she is asked.
4. She never touches anything she can move instead.
5. She is on your side of the joke, and she is still going to collect.

If you would rather not voice her, **use Ambrose.** He posts the lots, takes the
bids and reads out the balances. Connie is more effective scarce, and an arc where
the party only deal with the clerk is an arc where her eventual appearance means
something.

## OPEN questions

- **Whether the readings are true.** Keep it open. Nobody at the table should be
  able to settle it.
- **Whether Ambrose thinks she was right about the two.**
- **What the party's crew from Session 10 are now.** They were seized in the same
  lot. Smaller books of their own, worked off against wages, is the warm answer
  and keeps the named NPCs.
- **Who originally hired the party to hijack the *Verdant Zephyr*, and what the
  cargo was.** Unrelated to Connie, still unclaimed, still missing somewhere in
  the High City. Available to any GM who wants it.
- **The underwriters' claim file**, and who might one day read it.

**Settled, and not to be reopened at the table:** the Book opens at 12,000 gp and
the figure never moves. If the players ever see it move for any reason other than
the arithmetic, the whole device dies.
