<summary>
A passionate love letter from "Al" to "Jyl," filled with longing, references to secret meetings, and guilt about betraying those closest to them.
</summary>

<paragraph>
The letter spans five pages of fine paper, written in an elegant hand. "Al" writes to "Jyl" with barely restrained passion, describing nights stolen at social gatherings while others were distracted by cards and wine. He speaks of the burden of maintaining propriety in public while his thoughts are consumed by her. There are oblique references to "my household" and "your mother's expectations"--never naming names, but clearly acknowledging that both have much to lose. He mentions a specific upcoming occasion ("the harvest assembly") where he hopes they might find another moment alone. The letter closes with protestations of devotion and a plea that she destroy his correspondence, "for both our sakes." He signs it simply "Al."
</paragraph>

<full_letter>
My dearest Jyl,

I write to you by candlelight, as I seem to do so often now, when the house has gone quiet and I am left alone with my thoughts. They turn, as they always do, to you.

Three days since the Thornwood reception. Three days since I watched you across the room, laughing at something young Caelen said--some merchant venture or another, I'm sure, the boy speaks of nothing else--and I thought I might go mad with wanting to be the one at your side. Instead I stood by the wine table, making tedious conversation with the guild representative about timber prices, and counting the minutes until I might reasonably excuse myself.

You wore the green that night. You know the effect it has. I suspect you chose it deliberately, and I cannot decide if that makes you cruel or kind. Perhaps both. Perhaps cruelty and kindness are the same thing, when they come from you.

I should not write such things. I know this. Every letter is a risk, and I have begged you often enough to burn my words after reading them. Yet I cannot seem to stop. The alternative is silence, and silence is worse. At least on paper I can speak the things I cannot say aloud--not at gatherings, not with my household present, not anywhere that proprietry demands I be the man they expect me to be.

Do you know what I was thinking, that night at the Thornwood reception? I was thinking of the autumn, two years past, when your mother hosted that interminable dinner and you found me in the garden, escaping the third course. We spoke of nothing consequential--the weather, the vintage, whether the cook had been too generous with the pepper. And yet something changed between us in that conversation. I have replayed it a thousand times and I still cannot identify the moment. Only that I went into that garden as one man and emerged as another, and I have not been free of you since.

Your mother, incidentally, suspects nothing. I sat beside her at the Ashward dinner last month and she spoke warmly of her hopes for your future--some suitable match, I gathered, from a family with mainland connections. I nodded along and made appropriate noises of agreement while my stomach turned to ice. The thought of you belonging to someone else, of standing at your wedding and offering congratulations while my heart splits in two--

I cannot think of it. I will not.

Instead I think of the Spring Tide, and the back hallway where we stole five minutes during the Midsummer gathering. I think of your hand in mine, hidden beneath the table at the Lord's Council dinner, while I debated fishing rights with that insufferable Thassaril steward. I think of the letters you have sent me--fewer than I would like, but I understand, I do, the risks you take are greater than mine in some ways--and how I keep them locked in a box that supposedly contains estate documents.

My daughter asked me yesterday why I seemed distracted. I told her I was worried about the timber contracts. She accepted this, because she has no reason not to, and because she thinks me dull. I am grateful for that. Better she think me dull than suspect the truth. She speaks of you sometimes, you know. "Jyl and I are going to the Spring Tide this evening." "Jyl says the new wine shipment is exceptional." "Jyl thinks I wager too much."

She trusts you. She has no idea that her father--

I cannot finish that sentence. Some truths are too sharp even for private correspondence.

The harvest assembly approaches. I have already confirmed my attendance, though I have no particular interest in agricultural matters. Lady Seraphine hosts this year, I understand. Your mother. I will stand in her home, accept her hospitality, make conversation about yields and weather patterns, and all the while I will be watching for you. For some moment when we might slip away. For five minutes, or ten, or however long the stars grant us before duty calls us back.

Is this what my life has become? Stolen moments and careful letters and the endless performance of being someone I no longer am? I used to think myself an honest man. Strict, perhaps--my daughter would say tedious--but honest. Now I am a liar in my own home, and the worst part is that I cannot bring myself to regret it. Given the choice between honesty and you, I choose you. Every time. Without hesitation.

I should close this letter. It has already grown longer than is wise. But I find myself reluctant to set down the pen, because setting it down means returning to the world where I cannot speak your name with the weight it deserves.

Burn this letter. I know you will not--you never do, despite my pleading--but I must ask regardless. For both our sakes. If anyone were to find these pages, to read what I have written here, the consequences would be... I cannot bear to enumerate them. You know them as well as I do. My marriage. My reputation. My daughter's standing. Your mother's position. Everything we have both worked to maintain would crumble, and for what? For words on paper. For the indulgence of a man who cannot keep his heart where it belongs.

And yet I keep writing. And you keep reading. And we both keep pretending, in public, that we are nothing more than polite acquaintances, that your friendship with my daughter is the whole of our connection. Perhaps we have both gone mad. Perhaps this is simply what madness feels like--this constant oscillation between terror and longing, caution and recklessness, the life I am supposed to live and the life that consumes my every waking thought.

The candle burns low. The house remains quiet. Tomorrow I will rise, dress in the garments of a respectable lord, and attend to timber contracts and estate management and all the dreary machinery of my position. I will be Lord Maevathar, eldest of the island's nobles, father, husband, pillar of propriety.

But tonight, in these pages, I am only yours.

Until the harvest assembly, then. Until I can see you again, if only across a crowded room. Until some merciful chance grants us another stolen moment.

Yours, in everything that matters,

Al
