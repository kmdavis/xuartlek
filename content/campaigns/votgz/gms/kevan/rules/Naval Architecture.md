How airships fly, how big they can get, and why they can or can't pass a portal.

This is setting reference rather than table rules. Nothing here needs a die roll, but
the gauge ceiling and the class table decide what ships can show up where.

## The Levitation Ring

Every airship flies by means of one or more **levitation rings**.

A ring is a single solid hoop of forged iron, banded and pinned with brass, its inner
face carved with runes. It stands upright, perpendicular to the deck, and **completely
encircles the hull** like a wheel around an axle. It does not spin.

**The hull does not sit in the middle of the ring. It sits at the bottom of it.** The keel
and the inner face of the hoop's lower arc are bound tightly together, so a ring rises
almost entirely above the hull and reaches barely a hand's breadth below the keel. This
is why a ring is **part of a ship's landing gear**: set down, the hoop takes the ground
before anything else does, and it is built to. It is also the first thing lost in a
grounding.

Only part, though. One hoop is one line of contact, so a single-ring hull cannot stand on
its ring alone without tipping fore or aft, and needs folding struts, skids, or water to
steady it. Hulls with two or more rings stand on their own hoops unaided, which is one more
quiet advantage of splitting the lift.

**The landing shoe.** A bare hoop's lowest arc is a knife edge, and a ship standing on two
knife edges would roll. So the lowest arc of every ring carries a **flared bronze-shod
landing shoe**: the hoop's section widens athwartships into a flat-bottomed pad a few feet
across, centred under the keel. Minimal support, but enough that a landed ship sits stable
instead of rolling on her rims. Seen from ahead it reads as a small flat foot at the bottom
of the circle; from the side, as a low flange under the blade.

**Field shoes.** A paved apron is forgiving and most shoes are built for one. A hull that
sets down in stubble, mud and cart ruts carries the heavy version: the pad is half again as
wide, bronze over an iron sole plate, and carries a **screw-adjustable pad at each end of
the flange**. A few turns of a capstan bar at either end takes up a foot of slope, or lifts
the flange clear of a buried stone, and the ship stands level on ground that was never
meant to hold her. They are heavy, they need greasing, and they are the mark of a hull that
works unprepared country -- no liner carries them.

**The prop door.** Two rings hold a ship steady on the flat, but broken ground gives
uncertain bearing, and a hull can rock on a single good shoe. Freighters that land rough
solve it with the openings they already have: a **cargo door on each side amidships**,
immediately abaft the wing pivots, hinged at the sill and **hinged again across its own
middle**, so that it folds double to shut. That fold is the whole trick, and it is why the
doorway stands only half as tall as the ramp is long: a plank long enough to reach the
ground would never stow flat in the ship's side unfolded. Lowered, the two leaves open out
into one long ramp. Reinforced for the job, it gets an iron-shod toe and a pair of **pinned
strut legs that lock it at any angle**, so it bears weight instead of swinging. Drop the downhill door, pin the struts, and the ship stands a
tripod on a hillside -- then walk the cargo down the same door. It replaces the folding
skids older hulls carried, does the work of a third leg, and earns its keep twice.

**How many decks a hull can carry, and which one the door serves.** Depth follows length at
about one to six, and that depth is the whole budget. Take off three and a half feet of
bulwark standing above the weather deck, a foot of beams and planking for each deck, and a
knee-deep bilge of roughly three feet, and whatever remains divides into decks of about
seven feet -- enough for a loaded man to walk upright, which is the only figure that
matters. A 120 ft sloop has fourteen feet to spend and so carries ONE tall deck; a 140 ft
tramp has fourteen and a half after her deeper bilge and carries TWO at seven foot three.
The rule is unforgiving: a hull that wants another deck must get longer, never deeper.
Which deck the cargo door opens onto is then fixed by the ramp, not by choice. The door
folds double, so its ramp runs twice the doorway's height, and a ramp only lands at a slope
a barrel can be walked down -- about one in three. A six-foot door gives a twelve-foot ramp
reaching ground four feet below its sill, which is the sole of the LOWEST cargo deck and
nothing higher. Serving a deck seven feet further up would want a thirty-seven-foot ramp
folding into an eighteen-foot door, which no ship's side can stow. So the door always opens
on the bottom cargo deck, barrels roll straight in off the carts, and everything stowed
above it goes down through the main hatch on the derrick's fall.

The rest of the hoop is held off the planking by **two to eight radial spars** of
iron-strapped oak. Four to six is usual. Sloops often manage with two; men-o-war usually
carry eight. The count is driven by the ring's size and by how much redundancy the owner
wants, so a hull carrying its whole weight on a single ring tends toward the top of the
range whatever its size. Every spar lies flat inside the plane of its own ring, like the
spokes of a cartwheel, and runs out from the hull at a right angle to the keel. The spars
pass through braced brass collars in the hull sides and run down to an **arcane engine**
in the hold, which is the thing that actually powers the ring.

Inside the hoop the enclosed air holds a faint translucent veil of pale blue light, and
the runes glow the same colour. You can see straight through it. When the ring is
unpowered the iron is dark and cold and the runes are only carved grooves.

Rings on larger and later hulls are **faired**: shaped as an aerofoil in section rather
than a plain round bar. On amphibious hulls the submerged lower arc is sheathed in bronze
and built **stout rather than fine** -- there is no call for speed through water, so it is
made to survive a knock, not to cut.

A ring never fouls. No weed, no barnacles, no slime, at any depth and however long the hull
sits in a berth, because the power running through the iron kills anything that tries to
settle on it. A fouled ring means a dead engine, and it means the ship has been sitting
without power for a long while.

**Rings lift. They do not propel.** All forward motion comes from sail, or on the newest
hulls from a steam-driven propeller. This single fact drives every design decision below.

## Steering, Rails and Boats

**No airship has a rudder, with exactly one exception.** A rudder needs moving fluid to bite, and a
ship drifting with the wind has none: she and the air travel together, so a blade hung in it does
nothing at all. The EXPRESS STEAMER is the sole hull that breaks the rule, and only because she
breaks the underlying condition -- her screw throws a hard stream of air aft, and a blade standing in
that stream has something to push against. So she alone carries an AIR RUDDER: a tall narrow blade of
light timber ribs and doped canvas, hinged on an iron post immediately ABAFT the screw, in its wash.
It is worth having. Under power she comes round inside her own length and holds a heading in gusts
that would have a freighter sawing at her wings all watch, and a good part of her reputation for
keeping schedule is really the rudder. Her wheel works it through the same chain runs as the wings,
with a CLUTCH on the drum to throw the rudder out of the circuit when the screw stops -- because the
moment the screw stops the rudder is dead weight, and she steers by wings alone like everything else
in the sky. Every few years a yard hangs a rudder on a sailing hull, sells it on the strength of the
steamer's reputation, and is quietly surprised when it does nothing whatever.

Otherwise, ships steer by trimming their WING
SAILS: broad swept canvas wings, one per side, rooted low on the hull's flanks in heavy
hinged tabernacles, swept aft about thirty-five degrees. Trimmed together they drive;
trimmed against each other they turn the ship.

**Cargo hulls hinge their wings to swing up.** A wing rooted forward of a cargo door lies
straight across the working ground: with the door down and the ramp out there is nowhere to
stand, let alone room to walk a barrel. So freighters step each wing on a heavy iron PIVOT
with a pinned locking quadrant and swing it fully upright before they open up -- raised and
stowed above the rail, held by a chain tackle led to the mast. Wings up is the first job on
setting down and the last undone before lifting, and a tramp standing in a field with her
wings up is a tramp open for business. Warships carry no such fitting; nothing on their
flanks needs the room.

**The trim helm.** Nobody hauls those wings by hand. Every flying ship carries a helm
platform aft with a SINGLE SHIP'S WHEEL and a compass binnacle.

**Passenger hulls put the helm on the roof.** A freighter's helm stands aft on her deckhouse and
nobody minds, because the only people aboard are working. A liner cannot have three hundred
passengers strolling through her steering station, and a coxswain cannot con a ship through a crowd
of top hats. So a passenger hull carries her helm on an open platform ON TOP OF the passenger
gallery, at its forward end, reached by its own ladder: the coxswain looks straight out over the bows
above everyone's heads, and the only people who can reach him are the ones who are meant to. It is
open to the weather, which the companies describe as bracing and the coxswains describe otherwise.

**How a liner is divided inside.** A freighter is a box with holds in it. A liner has to keep three
kinds of people apart, and she does it with two full-depth bulkheads rather than with decks:

- **Forward of the first bulkhead, at a fifth of her length: cargo.** One tall hold running the full
  depth, under the forward hatch, carrying mail sacks, corded trunks and nothing else. This is the
  only cargo space in the ship.
- **Aft of the second bulkhead, at two thirds of her length: engineering.** Also one tall space the
  full depth, because her machines want height. Both of her engines live here -- the arcane levitation
  engine that holds her up and, uniquely in the fleet, a coal-fired boiler and steam engine that drive
  her along. Cold blue glow and hot orange firedoors in the same compartment.
- **Between the two: two decks, passengers above, crew below.** Cabins off a centre passage up top,
  panelled and carpeted; berths, mess table and galley down below, scrubbed bare. No passenger goes
  down there and no hand comes up in working rig.

Because there are only two decks in all that depth, both are tall and airy by shipboard standards --
not the cramped tween decks of a freighter. The companies charge for that headroom.

The passenger door opens low in her side, which is a problem, because the cabins are on the upper of
the two decks. The answer is a **tall entrance hall** just abaft the forward bulkhead, spanning both
decks, with a varnished companion stair rising through it. A passenger steps in at the bottom and
walks up to her cabin without ever setting foot on the crew's deck. It is the most expensive-looking
room in the ship and it exists to solve a plumbing problem. The wheel works both wings
DIFFERENTIALLY through a chain-and-quadrant run in covered deck channels: put the wheel
over and one wing sheets in while the other lets out, turning the ship, exactly as a
wheel-and-rudder would. It looks like an ordinary helm and handles like one; it just steers
canvas instead of a rudder. Larger hulls add a COLLECTIVE TRIM LEVER beside the wheel that
angles both wings together to climb or dive. Steering each wing by hand at its tabernacle
is the emergency fallback when the chain run fails. (Amphibious hulls carry a true rudder
on the same wheel as well, for the water.)

**Ascent and descent.** Altitude is ring power. The helm carries an ASCENT LEVER that
commands the engine's output to the ring -- direct-linked on small ships, a telegraph to the
engineer on large ones -- and the ship rises or settles as her lift passes above or below
her weight. This is the only method that works at a hover, and hovering is most of the job:
vertical landings, holding at a cargo tower, creeping into harbour. Underway, larger ships
also carry a COLLECTIVE TRIM LEVER that angles both wings together to climb or dive on the
move -- faster than the ring answers, but dead weight at zero airspeed. The split is a
submarine's: ring power is ballast, wings are dive planes.
Ring response is SLOW -- the crystal spools over long seconds, so the lever sets altitude
and the wings do the dodging. And a ship at hover weighs exactly her lift, so unloading
makes her RISE: working a cargo tower means bleeding ring power continuously as the hold
empties, which is why tower berths are skilled, expensive work.

**Losing lift.** A ring's lift DECAYS LINEARLY to nothing when power stops. Cut the feed --
by choice, by a dead engine, or by a severed conduit -- and the ship does not drop; she
**sinks steadily** as the lift bleeds off, giving the crew a known, gentle glide down to
whatever surface is below, ideally a flat one. This is how civilians land: reduce power and
ride the sink. It is also the safe failure mode, and it is why a stalled engine is a
nuisance rather than a disaster.
A ship can sink FASTER by driving the ring in REVERSE, but the reverse control is a
military fitting; civilians outside racing never install it and simply cut power to come
down. Racers fit it to dive on a mark.
The one lethal failure is **a broken ring**. If the hoop is shattered, cracked through or
fragmented, its lift does not decay -- it STOPS, at once and completely, with appropriately
dramatic results. A multi-ring hull that loses one ring sags and limps on the rest; a
single-ring hull that loses its ring is no longer a ship, which is the whole argument for
redundancy and for a single-ring hull being able to float.

**Rails without gaps.** A flying ship's crew lives a mile above the ground, and they treat
a gap in the rail the way sailors treat fire. Every deck carries a continuous bulwark
topped with HAMMOCK NETTING -- the crew's bedding stowed in the rail, navy-fashion -- from
stem to stern, broken only by a single gated gangway port. A ship with open rails is a
ship whose crew is either desperate or dead.

**No boats on pure airships.** A rowboat is dead weight on a ship that never touches
water. Crews reach the ground at a mooring, by rope lift, or not at all. Amphibious hulls
carry real ship's boats, because they float among them; the greatest capital hulls may
carry a ring-fitted launch, at ring prices.

## The Cube Law

A ring's lifting power goes as the **cube of its diameter**.

Doubling a ring's diameter multiplies its lift eightfold. The practical consequence is
that **fewer, larger rings always beat more, smaller ones**: four rings of half diameter
give only half the lift of one full-diameter ring.

**Lift is rated in tons of MASS**, not of weight. That distinction is the whole reason the
ratings are worth anything: a ring holds up the same mass whatever the local gravity, so a
hull that flies laden on one world flies laden on all twelve, and a rating stamped on a
plate in the High City means the same thing everywhere in the empire.

The anchor to remember is that **a 66-foot ring lifts 1,000 tons**. Everything else follows:

```
Lift in tons = Σ  1000 × (ring diameter in ft ÷ 66)³
```

That figure is the ship's maximum all-up mass: hull, rig, rings, crew, stores, armament and
cargo together.

**Equivalent ring** is the single ring that would do the same job:

```
Equivalent ring in ft = 66 × (total lift in tons ÷ 1000)^⅓
```

Comparing it to the hull's length gives a **density index** that tells you what kind of ship
you're looking at:

| Index (equiv. ring ÷ LOA) | Ship type |
|---|---|
| ~0.44 | fast warship: armoured, but deliberately kept light |
| ~0.45 to 0.50 | ordinary unarmoured merchant or courier hull |
| ~0.53 | armour at capital scale |
| ~0.63 | a hold packed full of dense cargo |

## Portal Gauge

A ship's masts are all built to strike. The mechanism varies by design: masts telescope
down, sweep fore or aft on hinged tabernacles, or bend. Struck, sails brailed and gear
stowed, **no part of a ship projects outside the circle of its own levitation ring**.

So a ship's **portal gauge is simply its largest ring diameter**, and that is also its
frontal silhouette. Nothing else about the hull needs measuring.

**Every portal has the same aperture: 120 ft.** Not because that is a physical limit, but
because every known portal was built and is maintained by **The High City**, to one
standard, and the High City holds the technology. There is no haggling over a wider gate
and no gate that opens further on a good day. 120 ft is the ceiling on inter-world trade
and the ceiling on inter-world war, and it is a political fact as much as an engineering
one.

That standardisation is worth remembering as a thing that could change. Unknown
civilisations, and possibly some known but secretive factions, may well have portals of
their own, built to nobody's standard. Nothing in the empire's shipping has been designed
with that in mind.

Consequences:

- **Men-o-war cannot transit and never will.** They stay in their home skies for defence
  and local power projection.
- **Over-gauge hulls can only ever be built where they will serve.** This is why every
  world in the empire needs its own yards, and why the men-o-war built at
  Maestri d'Ascia on Xuartlek's back will never leave it.
- **Merchant profit is capped by the aperture, not by the ship.** A bulk freighter at
  gauge is the largest cargo anyone can move between worlds. Anyone who could widen an
  aperture by ten feet would be very rich and very dangerous.

## Classes

Lift is the all-up mass the rings will hold, from the formula above. Cargo is what is left
of it after hull, rig, crew, stores and armament.

| Class | LOA | Rings | Spars per ring | Gauge | Lift, all-up | Equiv. ring | Index | Cargo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Courier sloop | 110 ft | 1 × 50 ft | 2 | 50 ft | ~435 t | 50 ft | 0.45 | light |
| Naval sloop | 120 ft | 2 × 45 ft | 2 | 45 ft | ~635 t | 57 ft | 0.48 | none |
| Tramp freighter | 140 ft | 2 × 55 ft | 4 | **55 ft** | ~1,160 t | 69 ft | 0.50 | ~500 t |
| **Grim Zephyr** | **206 ft** | **1 × 95 ft** | **8** | **95 ft** | **~2,980 t** | **95 ft** | **0.46** | **~1,200 t** |
| Express steamer | 200 ft | 2 × 85 ft | 4 | 85 ft | ~4,270 t | 107 ft | 0.54 | ~1,200 t + coal |
| Frigate | 250 ft | 4 × 90 ft | 4 | **90 ft** | ~10,150 t | 143 ft | 0.57 | none |
| Bulk freighter | 220 ft | 2 × 110 ft | 6 | **110 ft** | ~9,260 t | 139 ft | 0.63 | ~7,000 t |
| Man-o-war | 600 ft | 3 × 220 ft | 8 | 220 ft | ~111,000 t | 317 ft | 0.53 | none |

Note the spar counts against the ring counts: the Grim Zephyr carries eight spars because
there is no second ring to fall back on, while the frigate splits its lift four ways and
needs only four spars to a hoop.

**The tramp freighter is the cheapest lesson in the table.** Two 55 ft hoops lift 1,160 t
where one 70 ft hoop lifted 1,190 t -- call it the same ship -- but her gauge falls from
70 ft to 55 ft, she stands on her own feet without skids, and losing a ring no longer means
losing the ship. She pays for it in spars, collars, conduit and a second engine tap. Every
owner who has done the sum has re-ringed, which is why an old single-ring tramp is a sign of
a hull nobody has spent money on in thirty years.

**The bulk freighter is the opposite pole, and the two of them together define the trade.**
She lifts 9,260 tons on two 110 ft hoops and carries fourteen times the tramp's cargo. She
can deliver it to perhaps a dozen places in the world. Her hoops measure 110 ft against the
standard 120 ft aperture, which makes her **the largest hull that can still pass a gauged
portal** -- five feet of clearance to either hand and no margin whatever for a larger
refit. Re-ring her and she is stranded for good in whichever ring of the world she happens
to be standing in.
Her sides are unbroken: no cargo doors, no ramps. Her shoes are plain flanges without
adjuster pads, so she cannot stand on a slope, cannot load off a cart, and cannot set down
anywhere but a prepared and level apron. She loads vertically through her hatches, and
though she carries her own booms for light work, her real cargo moves under the port's shore
gantries. Without them she cannot work cargo at all.
**What she actually carries, and it is not loose.** "Bulk" describes her tonnage, not her
cargo. Loose cargo -- ore, gravel, grain, coal -- is the rare exception, and every hand who has
worked it hates it: a hold full of loose ore has to come out the way it went in, by hand, shovel
by shovel, into baskets, up through a hatch, and a gang can spend a week emptying what a gantry
filled in a day. There is no machine in the world that will reach down into a hold and lift a
heap.
So NINE TENTHS of what she lifts is CASED: crates, barrels, casks, bales and sacks, stacked on
timber dunnage, chocked and wedged and lashed, every piece sized so that two men can shift it
and a sling can lift a dozen at once. A cased hold empties in a day. Loose cargo moves only
between the few ports that keep the plant for it -- grabs, chutes, a tipping cradle -- and it
pays a premium for the privilege, which is why most owners never bother.

So the tramp trades reach for capacity and the bulk freighter trades capacity for reach:
five hundred tons to any field flat enough to stand on, or seven thousand to a paved apron
with cranes. Neither can do the other's work, and no hull yet built has managed both.

**She cannot stand on her own feet, and does not need to.** A tramp carries wide field shoes
with screw pads because she sets down in stubble. The bulk freighter carries nothing of the
kind -- her rims take a plain narrow bronze wear-band and no adjustable gear at all -- because
every apron licensed to work her has a **CRADLE**: a pair of shaped stone-and-timber saddles
bedded into the apron, spaced to her hoops, that receive the rims and hold her steady while
she is worked. Cradled, she sits rock solid under a full gantry lift. Off a cradle she has
nowhere to stand at all: a bare rim on flat stone is a single line of contact, and it will
gouge, roll or topple. That is why her sailing list runs to a dozen ports and always will,
and why an unscheduled landing is a salvage operation rather than an inconvenience.

**And her wings never fold.** A tramp hinges hers to clear her own ramp; the bulk freighter
has no ramp and no door, so her wings are FIXED in rigid iron sockets and stay spread even in
port. She carries **FOUR of them, two to each side** -- a forward pair and an after pair, set
between the hoops -- because no single pair of buildable span can turn nine thousand tons.
The aprons work around them: a gantry licensed for bulk hulls is built tall enough to swing
its load clear of a spread wing, which is one more piece of shore plant her trade demands and
the tramp's does not.

**Two masts, and still under-canvassed.** At 220 feet she steps TWO masts, each with a single
square sail, where every smaller merchant makes do with one. It is not much even so. Her
hoops do the lifting and her wings do the turning; canvas only drives her along, and there is
not enough of it for her tonnage. Bulk freighters are the slowest hulls in the sky and their
owners have never once cared, because nothing else will move seven thousand tons at any
speed at all.

## The express steamer, and the only fire in the sky

**She is the one hull that burns anything.** Every other ship in the fleet is lifted by her
rings, turned by her wings and driven by her canvas, and that is the whole of it. The express
steamer adds a coal-fired boiler below decks and an air SCREW at her stern, and she is the only
class that does. The rings still carry her weight -- fire has nothing to do with lift -- but the
screw gives her a speed no sail can hold, and speed is the entire product she sells.
**So she carries people.** Where a freighter has holds she has cabins, a saloon and a mail room,
and she runs to a published schedule rather than to wherever the cargo is. More passengers cross
the sky in steamers than in every other class together. Her freight is light and valuable --
mail, bullion, medicines, things that spoil -- about 1,200 tons of it, and then the bunker coal
on top, which pays nothing, occupies space that would, and must be bought again at every port
that stocks it. A steamer's owner thinks about coal the way a tramp's thinks about weather.

**And every yard in the world is trying to abolish her boiler.** The reasoning is obvious
enough: the same power that turns a levitation ring ought to turn a screw. It has nearly worked
for forty years. Rune-cut crystals geared direct to a shaft, conduit-fed drums, counter-rotating
cages, a dozen patents and a dozen quiet retirements. Ring-power holds a hull up beautifully and
flatly refuses to push it along, and nobody has yet published a reason why. Every few years
another yard announces it has solved the thing at last; so far every one of them has gone back
to coal, and the trade has learned to enjoy the announcements.

**Her index gives her away.** Run the numbers and she comes out at 0.54 -- squarely in the bracket
the table reserves for **armour at capital scale** -- and she carries no armour at all. What she
carries instead is a boiler, an engine, a shaft, a screw and several hundred tons of coal, and it
turns out that machinery and fuel weigh very nearly what a warship's plating does. A dockyard clerk
reading her ring size against her length, with no other information, would take her for a
second-rate man-o-war. It is the standing joke of the trade that the fastest passenger ship in the
sky is built to the same ratio as a ship of the line, and for the same reason: both of them are
mostly heavy iron that does not pay.

**She needs a door, and this is why.** She does not lie alongside a pier -- nothing in this world
does. She settles onto a paved apron on her ring shoes with her keel a foot off the stone, and two
hundred passengers have to get down from her somehow. A freighter solves that with a cargo ramp a
barrel can be walked down; a liner cannot ask a lady in travelling dress to walk down a cargo ramp.
So every passenger hull carries a PASSENGER DOOR: a neat man-high doorway in her side, well forward
of her machinery, opening onto a FOLDING ACCOMMODATION STAIR -- a proper flight of timber treads with
stanchions and a rope handrail, hinged at the sill and stowed flat inside the doorway in flight. It
is a fraction of the size of a tramp's prop door and bears nothing but people; her sides are
otherwise unbroken, because a hole in a hull is a hole in a hull. Mail and trunks go through a small
hatch forward on the derrick's fall, not through the door, so that passengers and cargo never meet on
the same stair -- a distinction the companies advertise.

**And she carries no sails at all.** This confuses people who expect a ship to have canvas on her
masts, but the arithmetic is plain: the wings already drive her and turn her, and the screw drives
her harder than canvas ever could. A square sail would add weight aloft, windage, and a gang of
hands to work it, in exchange for nothing she does not already have. So her masts are BARE POLES --
signal halyards, a lookout's crosstree, the mail derrick, and nothing else. At any distance that is
how you tell a steamer from everything else in the sky: she is the ship with poles instead of
canvas, and a smudge of smoke over her.
She can afford the omission because of the wings. If her fire goes out or her bunkers run dry, she
trims her wings together and they drive her, exactly as they drive every other hull in the fleet.
She makes port late, slowly and to a chorus of complaints from her passengers, but she is never
helpless -- which is precisely why no owner has ever paid to hang canvas on her.

## The stack arbalest, and why nobody needs a cannon to kill a ship

**Cannon are new, expensive and military.** No merchant carries one and no yard will sell one
without a warrant. What a merchant carries, and has carried for two hundred years, is the
ARBALEST: a heavy deck-mounted crossbow on a pivot, cheap, silent, serviceable by two hands, and
perfectly adequate against the only threat she expects.

**And the pattern that matters is the STACK ARBALEST.** The problem was never the bolt, it was the
bow: a limb heavy enough to matter takes two hands and twenty seconds to wind back, which is one
shot per pass and not worth the deck space. No mechanism will draw such a bow quickly. So the yards
stopped trying to wind faster, and multiplied the bow instead.
A stack arbalest carries **FOUR COMPLETE BOWS**, stacked one above another at the front of a long
timber stock like the leaves of a cart spring. Each is a FULL-WIDTH pair of composite limbs with its
own string, its own nut and its own short track, and all four are wound and loaded in advance, at
leisure, hours before anyone expects a fight. The four sit in a slim iron-bound magazine frame that
slides in guides on the stock, with a toothed rack down its side and a sprung pawl riding on it.
Loose the bow that lines up with the groove; the pawl trips, the frame steps down a notch, and the
next drawn bow comes into line. Four bolts in a few seconds, from a weapon that needed no winding at
all in the moment.
It is still a crossbow, and deliberately so: one man shoulders the butt and sights along the groove
while a second works the long cocking lever. A spent bow is obvious at a glance -- slack string,
empty track -- so anyone looking at a stack can count what is left in it, including the man being
shot at.

**The rarer pattern is the DRUM ARBALEST**, and it is a rich ship's weapon: six bows spaced around
the rim of a revolving drum instead of stacked in a frame, indexing a sixth of a turn per shot. Two
more shots than a stack, and a worse bow for every one of them -- a drum's rim will only take short
limbs, and short limbs mean a weaker cast and less punch through canvas at range. Yards keep
building them and gunners keep asking for stacks. Where volume matters more than penetration, at
boarding range or against a wing already torn, the drum earns its keep; everywhere else the stack
does.

**Volume is the whole point, because one bolt does nothing.** A single bolt through a canvas wing
punches a hole, and a hole in a wing is nothing at all -- the ship flies on and a hand patches it
next watch. Sustained fire is different: it STITCHES A LINE of holes across a panel, the line of
holes becomes a tear, and the tear runs under flight load until the panel strips off its own
battens. One bolt is a repair. A whole drum emptied into one panel is a tear that runs
under load until the canvas strips off its battens, and a ship that cannot steer, cannot trim, and
cannot run.
Wings are also the easiest thing in the sky to hit: enormous, thin, and standing well clear of the
hull, so a shot that misses the ship still finds canvas. Gunners aim at nothing else. It is why
wings are built in replaceable battened panels, why every hull carries spare canvas and battens,
and why a good crew can re-skin a wing aloft -- badly -- in about an hour.

**What it costs, and it is the whole tactical shape of a sky fight.** Four shots is a few seconds of
fury followed by a very long silence. Reloading means drawing every bow back to full cock with the
lever, one at a time, and that is minutes of hard work by two hands with the weapon out of action --
so a ship gets ONE burst per pass and then nothing. The rack and pawl are fine work that hate cold,
damp and dust, and they live under a tarpaulin and get more attention than the engine.
So warships mount several arbalests and fire them in sequence, merchants mount one or two and fire
only to discourage, and every captain aloft counts the other fellow's slack strings. **An arbalest
is a threat that visibly runs out**, and fights in the sky are shaped around that: one pass, one
burst, then a long wary circling while both sides wind.
Against a levitation ring the whole business is futile -- forged iron ignores bolts entirely -- and
that asymmetry shapes every fight in the sky: **you cannot bring a ship down, only bring her to a
halt.**

### Reading the table

**A bulk freighter is twice the weight of a frigate thirty feet longer than it.** Armour
is a thin shell over a large box; cargo is the whole box, solid. A frigate's all-round
armour comes to something like 1,500 tons, and its guns, carriages, shot and powder to
another 270. The same hull filled with grain would carry several times that.

**A frigate's gauge is 40 ft narrower than the convoy it escorts.** Warships therefore
transit gates their own freighters cannot use, and can chase through apertures a bulk
hull would never fit. That asymmetry is free, and it is worth using.

**Ring count is a design choice, not a size.** Merchants buy the fewest, largest rings
that lift the cargo they mean to sell, because rings are the most expensive thing on the
ship. Navies split the same lift across more, smaller rings for **battle-damage
redundancy**: lose one ring in action and the ship sags rather than falls.

### Nothing that has to be struck may mount on a ring

Masts, spars and sails all step on the **hull**, never on the hoop or its spars. Anything
carried on the ring cannot be swung, telescoped or folded clear of the aperture, so a hull
rigged that way could never transit. It is a tempting piece of engineering -- the hoop is right
there, and it is the strongest structure on the ship -- and it is why the occasional
ring-rigged hull exists as a purely local craft that will never leave its own sky.

### Why nobody builds one enormous ring

Three reasons, any of which will do at the table:

1. The gauge ceiling.
2. A single ring is a single point of failure.
3. Structure. One ring lands the ship's entire weight on one frame station, and that
   station's **ring truss** is the most expensive component in the hull. A cracked truss
   is a shipyard job, not a repair under way.

## Making Port

Rings lift, so an airship at rest is still holding itself up. How a ship gets its cargo
in and out divides the merchant fleet in three:

- **Tramp freighters** are small enough to set down in a field and load off carts. They
  go anywhere, which is the whole point of them. Field shoes and a pair of reinforced
  **prop doors** let them do it on a slope, which is most fields.
- **Bulk freighters** are far too heavy to set down at all. They hover, and require a
  port fitted with **cargo handling towers** that reach up to a levitating hull. Only a
  handful of ports in any world are equipped, which concentrates bulk trade brutally.
- **Amphibious freighters** float. They settle into an ordinary water harbour and work
  cargo at any deep berth. No towers, no special berth, no equipped port at all.

An amphibious hull needs **harbour handling, not sea speed**. It descends into the water
inside the harbour or just outside it and then warps slowly up to the pier on its own lines
and capstans. Nothing about the hull or the ring's submerged arc has to be fast through
water, which is why the fairing on a ring is built to survive a knock rather than to cut.

### Ports below the waterline

An amphibious hull floats, so any deck low enough to submerge takes its weapon ports
underwater with it. **Every opening below an amphibious hull's waterline carries two
closures, and the inner one is the pressure boundary.** Never the outer one.

| | What it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Outer shutter** | A faired plate of bronze-backed oak sliding vertically in bronze guides, dropping into a rabbet so it closes dead flush with the planking. Worked by a rod from inboard through a stuffing gland. | Fairs the hull, keeps weed and debris out of the bore, takes the first of any knock. **Not a seal.** |
| **The bore** | A short bronze-lined tunnel through planking and frames, draining inboard to a small sump with a **drain cock**. | Lets the crew tell whether the shutter is leaking without opening anything. |
| **Inner door** | Heavy timber faced with tallowed leather over oakum, seating on a bronze frame, closed by four iron dogs on wedge cams. | **The watertight boundary.** Dry side, reachable, inspected before every landing. |

The reasoning is maintenance, not pressure. An outer lid seals well enough, because the sea
pushes it onto its seat, but its hinges and dogs live in the water where they foul, corrode
and get knocked, and nobody can reach them from inside. An inner door has the sea trying to
push it off its seat and needs proper dogs for it, but a hand can get to it, wedge it harder,
and see it fail.

A weapon on a submerging deck runs out through the bore on a sliding carriage and must be
hauled fully inboard before the inner door will swing. So the closing drill runs: unload and
run the weapon in, swing the inner door and set all four dogs, drop the outer shutter and
drive its wedges, then crack the drain cock and see what comes out. Skip the last step and the
first anyone knows of a bad shutter is water on the deck.

Two consequences worth keeping:

- **A weapon on a submerging deck can only be fought in the air.** Afloat, that battery does
  not exist.
- **This is why an amphibious hull has no cargo doors.** A hole big enough to pass cargo
  cannot be given a credible inner door, and nobody has managed one that survives a season.

On a shipwright's plate these openings are labelled **balistraria**. The crew call the ones
that go under **drowned ports**.

Note what an amphibious hull may **not** have: **cargo doors in the side**. Hull ports
would ruin the seaworthiness the design was bought at. Tramp and bulk freighters can
afford side ports precisely because they never float. An amphibious hull loads instead
through **vertical cargo trunks** running up through every deck, with a crane amidships
hoisting loads to the weather deck for a pier crane or for hand.

That third option is the entire commercial argument for an amphibious hull, and it costs
dearly. See the Grim Zephyr below.

### Single-ring hulls must be able to come down safely

One ring is one point of failure, and a ship that loses its ring becomes a boat. Every
single-ring hull in service is therefore either small enough to glide down under sail or
genuinely seaworthy. **Amphibious design is a ring-failure survival system** as much as a
cargo-handling one.

## Armament

**Cannon are new.** They are heavy, expensive, and in practice military only. Merchant
hulls, privateers and anything built more than a few years ago mount **arbalests**: giant
steel-limbed crossbows on pintle mounts, with cranked windlasses and bolt racks. See
[[House Rules & Rulings|Ship Weapons]].

Warships are armoured on **all six faces**, not just the sides as a sea-going ship would
be. An attack can come from above or below.

**The naval sloop carries no broadside.** Too small for one, she mounts **two heavy pivot
cannon** on centreline turntables -- one forward of the mast, one aft -- each deliberately
over-calibred for the hull, on a racer ring with a slide carriage and compressor screws.
The mounts are **high-angle**: the gun trains through a full circle and elevates steeply,
because an attacker may hold the sky above. She cannot fire below her own keel; the deck is
in the way, and that blind cone under the hull is the classic way to approach one.

**The frigate is the smallest hull that can mount a broadside**, and mounting one is most of
what she is for. Twelve ports a side in a single tier, cut through her armour, with bronze
cannon on truck carriages behind them. Four blades divide her side into **three equal bays,
and each bay carries exactly four guns** -- no shipwright would cut a gun port where a
levitation spar has to pass, so the battery is laid out to the rings rather than the rings to
the battery. Nothing forward of the first blade or abaft the last: those spaces belong to the
pivot guns and the quarterdeck. She keeps the sloop's two high-angle pivot guns on the
centreline as well, fore and aft, and she keeps the sloop's blind cone underneath her too.

**She is the deepest-hulled ship in the fleet, and she breaks the one-sixth rule to do it.**
A trader is built one sixth as deep as she is long because depth she cannot fill is depth she
is paying to lift. A warship has no freight rates to answer to, so a frigate is built **one
fifth** as deep as she is long -- fifty feet on two hundred and fifty -- and spends it on
three and a half decks: a tall gun deck for the battery, a marines' deck under it, and a low
orlop for shot, powder and stores. It is the deep slab side, not the guns, that makes her
recognisable end-on from a merchantman of the same length.

**Her armour shows.** A merchant hull is planked and painted; a frigate is plated, in
overlapping courses of riveted iron laid on like heavy scales. Fifteen hundred of her
forty-seven hundred tons of lift is armour and another two hundred and seventy is guns,
carriages, shot and powder, which is why her lift index is a dismal 0.44 and why her rig is
short and plain. She has the tonnage for either armour or cargo, and the navy chose armour.

**Four rings, for the same reason she is plated, and every one of them larger than she
needs.** A merchant carries the fewest and smallest rings that will lift her cargo, because
rings are the expensive part and surplus lift earns nothing. A frigate does the opposite. She
carries four 90-foot hoops -- an equivalent ring of 143 feet and better than ten thousand
tons of lift, against an all-up weight nearer six -- and the surplus is the entire point:

> Four rings of ninety feet lift 10,150 tons. **Three of them still lift 7,600**, which is
> more than she weighs. Shoot a ring clean off a frigate and she sags, trims down by that
> quarter, and keeps flying.

That is what "sags rather than falls" actually means, and it is why her lift index of 0.57 is
high for a warship while her *useful* index is dreadful. She is paying for four rings and
using three. A merchant captain looking at those numbers sees a criminal waste of hoop; a
post-captain sees the only reason his ship comes home with a hole in her.

Her four blades stand proud of the rail by about four fifths of her own depth, and her rig is
cut down to little more than that, so blades and mastheads reach into the same band of sky.
At distance she is recognisable by exactly that silhouette: **four evenly spaced blades,
stubby masts, and a deep plated slab of a hull** -- no towering hoop like a liner, no
tophamper like a trader.

**Her wings are the only soft thing on her, and every gunner knows it.** Shot and bolts
shred canvas and ignore forged iron, so a fight between warships is a contest to cripple the
other's wings and then take her, because neither can bring the other down. Warships
therefore carry **spare wing canvas** rolled and lashed on deck, and a boatswain's party
whose whole trade is bending on a new wing under fire.

**There is no such thing as a ship's boat.** A hull needs a levitation ring to fly and the
smallest ring anyone has made useful is the courier sloop's fifty-footer, in a hull of a
hundred and ten feet. Nothing smaller than that flies at all. So no ship in the sky carries a
boat, a launch, a cutter or a set of davits: there is no water to launch into and nothing
small enough to launch. Everything and everyone that leaves a ship in flight does so onto
another ship, or does not leave.

**So every hull that carries people carries a door low in her side.** A liner has her
passenger door and folding accommodation stair; a tramp has her two-leaf prop door on the
lowest cargo deck; and a warship has a **marines' hatch** -- an armoured door of riveted
plate, dogged shut in flight, set low in her side on the lowest full deck, well below the gun
ports and clear of the ring blades. A folding timber ramp stows flat inside the sill and drops
from it when she is down.

One hatch each side, because a frigate lands to put her marines ashore and two hundred men in
harness cannot go over a bulwark on ropes. It is also the only opening in a warship's armour
that is not a gun port, which is why it is the heaviest door in the ship and why the
boatswain's first job after an action is to check its dogs.

That single fact about boats shapes boarding, and therefore shapes warships. You cannot pull
alongside and row across. You must **grapple and bridge**: heavy grapnels on coiled lines to drag the
two hulls together, then long timber **boarding bridges** with rope hand-lines and iron claws
at their ends, stowed flat in cradles along the bulwark and swung out onto the enemy's rail.
Then the marines go across in single file, in the open, forty feet above nothing. A frigate
action ends with a boarding or it does not end at all, and the boarding is the worst part of
it.
